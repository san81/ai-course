A farmer asked how much fertiliser to use. We had no rule — only examples. So we guessed a line, measured how wrong it was, and hunted for a better weight.

Everything today hangs off that chain. Nothing is replaced; we only add links.

A real farmer has more than one dial 🌾

Last week we gave him fertiliser, then rainfall. A real season has far more than two things pulling on the yield:

🌾 fertiliser☔ rainfall🌡️ temperature 🪨 soil type🌱 seed variety📅 sowing date 🐛 pest pressure🧪 pesticide💧 watering schedule

Nine things that matter → nine weights:
yield = w₁×fertiliser + w₂×rain + w₃×temp + … + w₉×watering

And there is still only one loss. All nine numbers have to come down together — nudge the fertiliser weight and the watering weight may need to move too.

Now ask the same question about words 📖

A farmer has nine things that matter. How many does a machine need just to know every word in the dictionary?

Where we stopped, deliberately

Last week we found the best weight by trying values — 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.1 — and keeping the one with the lowest loss.

That is fine for one dial. For nine it is already painful. For 410 million it is hopeless.

And GPT-4 has roughly a trillion — the dictionary is barely 0.04% of it. Trying values would still be running when the sun burns out.

So: how do you find a trillion numbers?

Hold that question. We need two other things first — and then we spend the whole middle of today answering it. 🔖

Stage 1 — the alphabet

A fixed set of symbols. For a model, they are not letters.

First, chop it up ✂️

Step 1 of the four: the alphabet. A model does not read words — it reads tokens, a fixed set of chunks decided before training starts.

Common words are one token. Rare words get broken into pieces. Roughly: 1 token ≈ ¾ of an English word.

The question that matters

Who taught you grammar? 📐

Somebody did. A teacher, a textbook, a parent correcting you. Rules, written down, handed over.

Nobody did that for the model. No one wrote "a pronoun refers back to a noun" anywhere inside GPT.

Week 1: people spent twenty years trying to hand-write the rules. Expert systems. It failed, and the field froze twice.

So before we can look at grammar, we have to answer a different question: how does it learn anything at all?

Act 3 of 5 · ⚙️

How does it learn anything?

Nobody wrote the rules. So where did they come from?

⚠️ We leave words alone for ten minutes and go back to the farmer. Then we bring the idea straight back to language.

A story first

Lost on a hillside, in thick fog 🌫️

You are somewhere on a hill at dusk. The fog is so thick you can see your own boots and nothing else. You need to get down to the valley.

You cannot see the valley. You cannot see the path. You have no map.

But you can feel which way the ground tilts under your feet.

You just invented the algorithm 🥾

Feel the slope. Step downhill. Feel again. Repeat until the ground is flat.

That is gradient descent — and it is how every model in this course learns its weights.

You have seen this hill before 🌾

Last week we drew the farmer's loss for different weights. Turn it on its side and it is the same picture:

Same valley. The farmer's hill just happens to have only one direction to walk in.

So what is the hill, for language? 🏔️

The farmer had examples: fertiliser in, yield out, and a gap we could measure. Where does a language model get its examples?

Add one more weight and the hill becomes a landscape 🏞️

Two weights: w₁ runs one way across the floor, w₂ the other. Height is still the loss.

Each step is a fraction of the slope — so as the ground flattens, the steps shrink by themselves. That fraction is the learning rate. 🥾

Nothing new is being invented as this grows. It is the farmer's arithmetic, with more columns. 🌾

Too small, too big, just right 👣

That step size has a name: the learning rate. It is the single most important dial in training a model.

🥚 how fast do you learn?

The dip that is not the valley 🕳️

A local minimum: everywhere around you is uphill, so you stop — but you are not at the bottom. In the fog, you cannot tell the difference.

You already do this

The bakery, and the price of a sandwich 🥪

6 rupees each, 330 sold a day. Drop the price by 25 paise and 15 more people buy. How far do you go?

One cut: better. Two cuts: back where you started — you have gone past the top. Same walk, same overshoot, no equation anywhere.

A story first

The coach who only sees the final score ⚽

A goal is missed. Eleven players touched the ball. Whose fault was it?

Nobody told the coach. He only knows one thing: it did not go in.

So he works backwards. The striker shot wide — but the pass came late. The pass was late because the midfielder was out of position. He was out of position because the defender cleared it slowly.

Every player gets a share of the blame — and the share is not equal.

Backwards through the team 🔁

That is backpropagation: one number at the end, turned into a personal correction for every weight in the network.

So what does "downhill" mean with a trillion dials? 🧭

The people

The man who built it in 1958 🧠

Frank Rosenblatt builds the Perceptron — a machine that learns from examples by nudging weights. Exactly what we just described.

The New York Times reports it will soon walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence.

In 1969 a book proves what a single perceptron cannot do. Funding stops. The field freezes. Rosenblatt dies in 1971, aged 43.

He was not wrong. He was fifty years early — the idea needed data and computers that would not exist until 2012.

From one weight to a trillion 📈

The fog walk does not care how many weights there are. One step adjusts all of them at once.

Same walk. Same slope. Just a hill with a trillion directions.

The farmer, one thousand times wider 🌾

Week 1: two inputs, two weights, one multiply-and-add.

There is no new maths in here. There is just an enormous amount of the same maths.

Why it needs a GPU 🎮

One brilliant professor doing seventeen million sums, one after another — versus a thousand ordinary students doing seventeen thousand each, all at the same time. The students finish first. Every time.

A CPU is the professor: a few very clever cores. A GPU is the classroom: thousands of simple cores doing identical arithmetic in parallel.

Built for video-game graphics. It turned out that shading a million pixels and running a neural network are the same shape of problem.

Act 4 of 5 · 📐

Stage 3 — grammar

The rules it was never given. This is attention.

A story first

Five hundred people, and you hear your name 🎉

A wedding hall. Deafening. Music, a hundred conversations, children running. You have stopped even trying to listen.

Then someone across the room says your name.

You hear it instantly — and you have no idea what anyone else said.

Your brain scored every sound in that hall for relevance to you, and spent almost all of its attention on one. That is the entire idea, and it has a name.

Remember this sentence? 🐘

"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired."

Last week I showed you this line and said "the model knows". Today: how.

Step 1: every word asks, and advertises 🙋

A marriage bureau. The Query is what a family is looking for. The Key is the one-line summary on each profile. The Value is the actual person behind the profile. Match queries against keys — then you get the values.

Act 5 of 5 · ✍️

Stage 4 — writing a sentence

Four stages down. This is what it all builds to.

What is ChatGPT actually doing when it answers you?

Guess before the next slide. Is it looking things up? Thinking? Something else?

It is guessing the next word. That's it.

"Chennai is famous for its ___"

Pick one → add it to the sentence → ask again. Loop. Every essay, poem and program it writes comes out of this one loop.

Training and inference, at real scale ⚖️

This is why a model has a knowledge cut-off — and why correcting ChatGPT never teaches it anything.

From "next word" to something helpful 🎓

A pretrained model is brilliant and unusable. The last two steps are what make it polite, helpful and safe enough to ship.
