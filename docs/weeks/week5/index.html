You asked me privately

"What went into Jev, and could I use it?" 🤖

🧪 The work behind it

Jev itself was built by a company, TypeSafe, and trained with RLCD so its "how sure" numbers are honest.

The game around it was the real work: writing the boards, a free spelling check first, 78 test guesses, and tuning the 75% / 45% thresholds until it felt fair.

📅 For a study planner?

Mostly no: a planner has to write a plan. That's a job for a model like Gemini or Claude, which you'll call today.

Jev only picks from options you give it. It could do one small step, like "is this a revision plan, an exam plan or an assignment plan?"

✍️ Need it to write? A generative model.  ·  ☑️ Need it to choose from a list? A decision model.

A story

"Talk to the computer in English" 👩‍💻

1950s

Grace Hopper argued that people should give computers instructions in words close to English. Experts said computers could only understand numbers.

She built one of the first compilers anyway, and her English-like language shaped COBOL, which still runs banks today.

Today

You will write the instructions for a working app in plain English.

The language finally caught up with her idea.

Layer 1 · no-code

The instructions are the app 🧩

You sort complaints sent to a college hostel
helpdesk in South India. Students write in English,
Telugu, or a mix of both, often casually.

For each message, decide:
- category: the ONE best category.
- urgency: high = a safety risk … ; medium = … ;
  low = everything else.
- summary: what is wrong, in at most 12 words.
- reply: a warm, polite reply, at most 40 words,
  in the language they wrote in. Never promise a
  date or a time. Never make up names or numbers.
- needs_human: true if there is any safety risk…
