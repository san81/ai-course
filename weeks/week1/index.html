Deep dive

AI vs ML vs Deep Learning

Chennai to Bengaluru: "transport" is the goal (AI) · "vehicles" are one way that worked (ML) · the Shatabdi Express is the specific one winning today (deep learning).
Three clicks. Stress the nesting: all deep learning is machine learning, all machine learning is AI, but not the reverse. A chess program with hand-written rules is AI and is NOT machine learning. When people say "AI" today they almost always mean the innermost circle. Ask: "Is a calculator AI?" — good argument starter. Answer: by 1950s standards, yes. We keep moving the goalposts; this is called the "AI effect".

How old is the field of AI?

Younger than the internet? Older than your grandparents? Write down a year.

Make them actually write it down — commitment makes the reveal land. Take three guesses aloud. Most say 1990s–2010s. Then reveal: the field is older than the integrated circuit, older than most of their parents.

1950 — the question that started it all

Alan Turing asks: "Can machines think?" — and proposes a way to test it.

The Turing test is a WhatsApp game: you chat with two hidden players, one human and one machine. If you cannot tell which is which, the machine passes.

You played exactly this game 10 minutes ago. In 1950 it was science fiction.

Turing sidestepped the philosophy. Instead of "can it think", he asked "can it behave indistinguishably" — a question you can actually test. That move is why he matters. Note: Turing was persecuted for being gay and died in 1954 at 41. Worth mentioning briefly and respectfully — it humanises the history. Callback to the icebreaker: the class scored close to chance. In 1950 that was unthinkable.

1956 — AI gets its name

The Dartmouth workshop: a summer project to make machines "use language, form abstractions, and improve themselves."

They estimated significant progress in… 2 months. It took 60+ years. (click to reveal)

Ten researchers, one summer, funded proposal. The term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined here by John McCarthy — partly to distinguish the field from cybernetics. The two-month estimate is the first example of a pattern that repeats through this whole lecture: AI is consistently over-promised in the short term and under-estimated in the long term. Tell them to remember this when they read today's headlines.

1960s–80s — intelligence as rules

Making sambar strictly from a written recipe. It works — until the vegetables are different, and the recipe says nothing. Every amma's version has a rule the cookbook never wrote down.
This was called symbolic AI or "expert systems", and it genuinely worked in narrow domains: MYCIN diagnosed blood infections better than junior doctors; XCON saved DEC millions configuring computer orders. The fatal flaw is the "knowledge acquisition bottleneck" — human experts cannot write down everything they know. Ask the class: "Can you write the exact rules for recognising your mother's face?" Nobody can. That's the whole problem in one question.

The AI winters ❄️

AI funding follows the monsoon: seasons of plenty when promises grow, then long dry spells when they don't deliver. Two long dry spells so far.
Concretely: in 1973 the Lighthill report to the UK government concluded AI had failed to deliver, and British AI funding was gutted. In the late 80s the specialised Lisp-machine industry collapsed almost overnight when ordinary desktop computers caught up. Researchers stopped using the words "artificial intelligence" on grant applications. Ask the class directly: "Are we in a summer right now? What would a third winter look like?" Let them argue for a minute — no correct answer, and it plants a healthy scepticism.

1990s–2000s — flip the approach

You don't teach a child to spot a dog by listing rules ("four legs, fur, tail" — then is a cat a dog?). You point at many dogs. The child works it out.
This is the single most important conceptual shift in the lecture. Say it plainly: we stopped telling computers HOW to do the task and started showing them WHAT the answer looks like, letting them find the how. Quiet examples from this era that they use daily: spam filters, search ranking, product recommendations, credit-card fraud alerts. AI was already everywhere before ChatGPT. Next we make "the machine finds the rules" concrete — because right now it sounds like magic.

How does a machine actually "learn"? 🎲

1959. Arthur Samuel at IBM writes a program that plays checkers (draughts). Within a few years it beats him — its own creator.

He coined the term for what it did: machine learning.

But what does the program actually do? Three questions: what does it see, what does it store, and what makes it improve?

Set this up as a mystery you are about to solve. This 3-slide sequence is the conceptual heart of the first half of the lecture — do not rush it, budget 10 minutes. Everything later in the course (neural networks, attention, training GPT) is this same idea scaled up enormously. If they understand these three slides, Week 2 becomes easy.

1. What it sees, 2. what it stores

Key point: the machine does not "see" a board the way we do. It converts the position into a short list of numbers. That conversion — real world into numbers — is the first step of every AI system, then and now. In Week 2 we will do exactly this with words instead of board positions: a word becomes a list of numbers called an embedding. Same idea, bigger list. Ask: "If you were writing this, what else would you measure?" Take two suggestions — it makes them feel the design decision.

3. The score sheet — and the weights

THE key slide. Say it slowly: the program's entire "opinion" about checkers is a handful of numbers. Nothing more. Analogy that lands here: choosing a college. Placement record, fees, distance from home, hostel food — everyone weighs these differently. Your weights ARE your opinion. Two students with the same facts and different weights reach different answers. Flag it loudly: GPT-4 is this same score sheet with roughly a trillion weights instead of four. Every time you hear "model parameters" or "a 70-billion-parameter model", they mean these numbers.

How the weights improve: play, compare, adjust

Five clicks; narrate each one. This loop — predict, compare with reality, nudge the weights — is ALL of machine learning. Modern name: gradient descent. Same loop, calculus decides the size and direction of the nudge. Two things Samuel invented here that are still central: (1) SELF-PLAY — the machine plays itself, so it never runs out of training games. (2) It also memorised positions it had already evaluated — an early cache. He called it rote learning. Cricket analogy: net practice. Play the shot, see where the ball went, adjust the grip, repeat. A machine can do this lakhs of times a day, and never gets tired or bored. That patience — not cleverness — is its real advantage.

1997 — Deep Blue beats Kasparov ♟️

IBM's machine defeats the world chess champion. Front-page news everywhere.

  • But it won mostly by brute force: 200 million positions examined per second
  • Its scoring rules were hand-tuned by human grandmasters — closer to the rulebook era than to learning
  • Chess is big, but small enough to search. What about a game that isn't?
Nice contrast to make explicit: Samuel's checkers program LEARNED and was weak. Deep Blue barely learned and was strong, because chess is searchable with enough hardware. Kasparov's reaction is worth telling: he became convinced a human was intervening, because one move looked too "human". It was actually a bug — the machine picked a random move. Ends the slide on a question, which sets up AlphaGo.

2016 — AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol 🔴⚪

Go has more legal board positions than there are atoms in the universe. Brute force is impossible — you cannot search your way out.

  • AlphaGo learned: deep learning + Samuel's self-play trick, at enormous scale
  • Game 2, move 37: a move no human would ever play. Commentators called it a mistake. It won the game.
Move 37 is the emotional peak of this section — tell it properly. The commentators said "that's a very strange move", one professional left the room. Fifty moves later it was clearly the winning move. Lee Sedol said it made him rethink Go itself. The philosophical point: the machine was no longer imitating human play. It had gone past its teachers. This is what "learning from examples plus self-play at scale" buys you. Ten years earlier experts predicted Go was a decade away. The documentary is on the resources slide and it is genuinely moving — recommend it.

2012 — deep learning's big bang 💥

AlexNet wins the ImageNet contest by a huge margin. Why suddenly? Data (the internet) + compute (gaming GPUs) + better training tricks.

An assembly line for understanding: the first station spots edges, the next combines them into shapes, then ears and eyes, then "that's a dog". Nobody assigned the stations — they organised themselves from examples.
"Deep" simply means many layers stacked — many stations on the assembly line. Each layer has weights, exactly like Samuel's score sheet, and they are tuned by the same predict-compare-nudge loop. AlexNet had 60 million of them. The GPU detail matters and pays off two slides later: the researchers used two gaming graphics cards. Hardware built for rendering video games turned out to be exactly what neural networks needed. We will see why in Week 2. Error rate dropped from 26% to 15% in one year — in that field, unheard of.

Why now and not in 1970? Moore's Law 🖥️

1965, Gordon Moore: the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. Computing keeps getting cheaper and faster — not by adding, by doubling.

Like land prices near Bengaluru's Outer Ring Road: slow-looking at first, then suddenly astronomical. The phone in your pocket outcomputes all of NASA during the moon landing.
Doubling is deeply unintuitive — spend a minute on it. The rice-and-chessboard story works beautifully here: one grain on the first square, doubling each square. By square 64 it is more rice than the world has ever grown. Practical framing: the algorithms behind AlexNet were mostly known in the 1980s. They failed then because the hardware was thousands of times too slow and there was no internet-scale data. Same ideas, different decade, different outcome.

…and its AI cousin: scaling laws 📈

Discovery of the 2020s: make the model bigger, feed it more data, give it more compute — and it gets predictably better. No new idea required. Just scale.

Moore's law made compute cheap. Scaling laws turned cheap compute into intelligence.

Why this was a shock: normally in engineering you hit diminishing returns. Here, bigger kept working, and some abilities appeared suddenly at scale — arithmetic, translation, writing code — without anyone programming them in. These are called emergent abilities. This is also why AI became so capital-intensive: if the recipe is "spend more", the game shifts to whoever can afford the most GPUs. Good hook for the ethics discussion in Week 8. Honest caveat: there is live debate about whether scaling alone continues to pay off. Say that openly — it models good scientific humility.

What is ChatGPT actually doing when it answers you?

Guess before the next slide. Is it looking things up? Thinking? Something else?

Take three guesses aloud. Common answers: "searching the internet", "it has a database", "it understands the question". All wrong in an interesting way. Let the wrong guesses sit — the reveal is much more fun after commitment.

It is guessing the next word. That's it.

"Chennai is famous for its ___"

Pick one → add it to the sentence → ask again. Loop. Every essay, poem and program it writes comes out of this one loop.

Say plainly: it is autocomplete on your phone keyboard, scaled up beyond recognition. Show the loop physically — write a few words on the board, extending one word at a time. The obvious objection, and you should raise it yourself: "then how does it answer questions correctly?" Because to predict the next word really well across the entire internet, it had to absorb grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and style. Understanding, or something that looks a lot like it, is a by-product of getting very good at guessing. Whether that counts as real understanding is a genuinely open question — flag it, and say Week 8 revisits it.

Two consequences you will feel every day

  • Creativity is a dial. Always pick the top word → dull and repetitive. Sometimes pick a lower one → surprising and creative. That dial has a name: temperature.
  • Hallucination is not a bug in the usual sense. The model optimises for plausible-sounding, not for true. A confident wrong citation is the system working exactly as built.

Both come straight back in Week 2, and again in Week 3 when we learn to prompt well.

Callback to the icebreaker: the AI answers felt "too smooth, too balanced". That is literally the model steering toward high-probability words. Their instinct was correct and now they know the mechanism behind it. Practical warning they should write down: an LLM is most dangerous exactly where it is most fluent. It never sounds unsure. Verification is their job, and Week 4 is about that.

And it needn't be words at all 🖼️🎵

Break anything into small pieces, number them, and predict the next piece:

This is why one model can read, see, listen and speak. The word for those pieces is tokens — Week 2's first topic.

"Multimodal" is the jargon; do use the word, they will meet it constantly. Concrete examples they know: describing a photo, reading a handwritten note, voice mode, generating images, transcribing a lecture recording. The deep point: the transformer does not care what the pieces mean. Give it any sequence split into pieces and it will find the patterns. Proteins and weather data work too — which is how AI ended up in drug discovery and forecasting. Transition: "so what is this machinery? That's the last big idea of today."

2017 — the transformer: the T in ChatGPT

Before it, models read text strictly one word at a time, left to right — and kept forgetting.

Like watching a 500-episode serial and being asked in episode 500 what happened in episode 3. The early models simply forgot. Long sentences were hard, long documents hopeless.

The 2017 idea: stop reading one by one. Let every word look at every other word at once — and learn which ones deserve attention.

The old approach was called RNN/LSTM — mention the names in passing only. Two problems with reading one word at a time: information from early words faded away, and it could not be parallelised, because word 50 needs word 49 first. Slow to train. The transformer fixed both at once. That is why it took over completely in about two years. Coming up: what "attention" actually computes. This is the hardest part of today — tell them so, and tell them an intro is enough for now; Week 2 goes deeper.

🥚 🎵 …all you need is 🎵

Do the "wide" version out loud: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too wide." Everyone's interpretation flips instantly. That flip is what attention computes. This exact sentence is the classic example from the original paper's illustrations. Now the honest question your students will ask, and the next slide answers it: HOW does the machine know to draw the thick line to "animal"? Ask it yourself before clicking.

But how does it know? Step 1: words become numbers

Exactly like Samuel turning a checkers board into four numbers — just a much longer list. These lists are called embeddings.

Explicit callback to the checkers score sheet. Same move: real world → numbers. Meaning becomes position. Words used in similar contexts end up with similar number lists, so "dosa" sits near "idli" and far from "algebra". Nobody hand-assigned these; they emerged from training on huge amounts of text. Typical length: 4,000+ numbers per word in a modern model. Week 2 spends real time here — vectors, and why arithmetic on them is meaningful. Do not go deeper today; just plant it.

Step 2: every word offers three things — Q, K, V

A marriage bureau. The Query is what a family is looking for. The Key is the one-line summary on each profile. The Value is the actual person behind the profile. Match queries against keys — then you get the values.
Alternative analogy if the room prefers it: a library. Query = what you want, Key = the label on the spine, Value = the contents of the book. Where do Q, K and V come from? Each word's embedding is multiplied by three sets of WEIGHTS — the same word from the checkers slide — one set for Q, one for K, one for V. Those weight sets are learned during training by the same predict-compare-nudge loop. Say the reassurance out loud: "you do not need to compute this today. You need to know the machine compares what each word wants against what every other word offers." Week 2 shows the actual matrix multiplication.

Step 3: scores become percentages

Compare "it"'s Query against every Key. Better match → higher score:

"it" now carries 71% of the animal's meaning. That thick green line was these numbers all along.

Two bits of jargon you can name lightly: comparing Q with K is a dot product — a similarity score between two lists of numbers. Turning raw scores into percentages is called softmax. Say the names, do not dwell; they will meet them again and it helps to have heard them. Then the beautiful part: this happens for EVERY word simultaneously, not just "it", and it happens in dozens of parallel copies called heads — one head tracks grammar, another tracks who-did-what-to-whom, and so on. Then the whole thing repeats across dozens of layers, each refining the meaning further. That is a transformer. Everything else is engineering detail.

Why this changed everything

  • Parallel, not sequential → thousands of GPUs can work at once → we could finally train on the whole internet
  • Long memory → episode 3 is never forgotten; every word stays visible
  • One design for everything → text, images, audio, code, proteins
  • The paper: "Attention Is All You Need" — Vaswani et al., Google, 2017

📺 3Blue1Brown — Attention in transformers, visually explained — watch this before next week.

The first bullet is the one to stress, and it links back to Moore's law and forward to Week 2's GPU discussion: the transformer's real superpower is that it suits the hardware we already had. A brilliant design that could not be parallelised would have gone nowhere. The video is genuinely the best explanation available and it covers exactly the Q/K/V material we just introduced. Tell them it is 25 minutes and worth every one. Homework framing: watch it, and come to Week 2 with one question about it.

2018 → today: the LLM era

  • 2018–19 — GPT-1, GPT-2: transformer + huge text = surprising fluency
  • 2020 — GPT-3: scaling laws in action; size alone unlocks new abilities
  • Nov 2022 — ChatGPT: 100 million users in two months
  • 2023–24 — GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, open models; multimodal
  • 2025–26 — reasoning models and agents: AI that plans and uses tools (our Week 7)
Detail worth telling: OpenAI initially refused to release GPT-2 fully, calling it too dangerous for misinformation. Today it would be considered a toy. Capability moves fast; so does our sense of what counts as dangerous. ChatGPT was not a research breakthrough — the model existed already. It was an interface breakthrough. Putting a chat box in front of it changed the world. Good lesson for their capstone projects: usability is as important as capability.

The whole picture 🗺️

Walk the line left to right in about 60 seconds; they have seen every point already, this is consolidation. Then the AGI fragment. Define it simply: Artificial General Intelligence — a machine that can do essentially any intellectual task a human can, not just one narrow thing. Be honest and balanced: serious researchers disagree by decades on when, or whether. Some think scaling gets us there; others think a fundamental idea is still missing. Note that today's systems are still narrow in important ways. Ask for a show of hands: "AGI within your working lifetime — yes or no?" No commentary on the result. It plants a question they will carry for eight weeks.
